The Signature Difficulty

Phonetic to read — lexical to write

Vaskric is notorious for its lexical diacritics. Spelling is phonetic at the level of sound-to-letter mapping — every mark tells you exactly how to pronounce the word — but which diacritic a word carries is lexical. It must be learned per word, because the diacritic encodes history and meaning, not just pronunciation.

This is the inverse of French. In French you can often guess how a word is spelled from hearing it, but you can’t guess how it’s pronounced from seeing it. In Vaskric you can always pronounce a word you see, but you cannot always spell a word you hear.

❧ ❧ ❧

Three rules of the diacritic layer

1. Consonant diacritics are phonemic

A diacritic on a consonant creates a different word, not a variant pronunciation. The mark is not decorative; it distinguishes lexemes that would otherwise be homographs.

Word IPA Meaning
tak/tak/so
t’ak/tʲak/roof
sol/sol/floor
s’ol/sʲol/sun
nam/nam/name
ñam/ɲam/food

Every one of these pairs is a genuine minimal pair in the language. Confusing them is not a spelling error; it is a semantic error.

2. The circumflex marks a lexically unpredictable lost consonant

The circumflex (̂) signals a consonant that dropped historically — but which consonant, and when, must be learned per word. You cannot predict it from the modern form. The word is still fully pronounceable (the vowel is simply long); the ̂ is an etymological flag, not a sound.

Word IPA Meaning Lost consonant Older form
vós/voːs/voicekvoks
hót/hoːt/highshost
pát/paːt/pathnpant

Note that the circumflex is the one controlled exception to Vaskric’s phonetic principle. Every other diacritic maps to a sound. The ̂ maps to silence — a tombstone, not a phoneme.

3. Grave vs acute is lexical, not predictable

Both mark vowel quality, but which a word takes is memorised, not derived. Two words with the same base vowel may carry different diacritics and mean completely different things.

Word IPA Meaning
més/mes/month
mès/mɛs/more

A reader who knows Vaskric can pronounce both words instantly. A listener who hears /mes/ and /mɛs/ must still know the language to spell them correctly.

❧ ❧ ❧

The consequence

Vaskric is phonetic to read aloud — every mark tells you exactly how to say the word — but not phonetic to write from sound alone, because the choice of diacritic carries lexical and historical information that pronunciation does not reveal. You can always pronounce a Vaskric word you see. You cannot always spell a Vaskric word you hear.

This is what makes Vaskric notorious. Outsiders describe it as a language that “looks French but refuses to behave like French.” Learners describe the first year as “learning to read twice” — once for sound, once for diacritic.

❧ ❧ ❧

The shibboleth thesis

This asymmetry is not merely typological. It is not an accident of history, and it is not a harmless orthographic quirk that later generations learned to live with.

Under suppression — and the Vaskrins were a suppressed minority for much of their history — the community used the lexical diacritic layer as a semi-closed orthography. Insiders could read any text aloud fluently. Outsiders transcribing from speech could not reliably reproduce the written form, because the diacritic choice is lexical and historical, not derivable from pronunciation.

The diacritics thus functioned as a boundary marker: a way of keeping literacy semi-closed to the community while remaining fully speakable in daily life. Anyone could hear Vaskric. Only insiders could write it back.

This is the single most important sociolinguistic fact about the language:

The language’s signature difficulty is a survival strategy, not an accident. It is the inverse of French, and it is what makes Vaskric notorious.

The eastern comparison

Vaskoryŏ — the Eastern dialect — inherited the same diacritic principle, but adapted it into the Hangul script. The underlying asymmetry is preserved: reading is phonetic, writing is lexical. The Korean script only changes the shape of the marks, not their function.
