About This Project

The Vaskric Archive is a constructed language project — a work of linguistic worldbuilding. Vaskric and Vaskoryŏ are not real languages, and the Vaskrin people are not a real people. The history, the geography, the endangerment narrative, and the sociolinguistic drama are imagined.

What is real is the linguistic engineering. Every rule in this archive — the phonology, the morphology, the diacritic system, the register split, the loanword strata — has been designed with intent, and every inconsistency has been ironed out on purpose. The site exists so the language can be read, explored, and studied outside of a design document.

❧ ❧ ❧

Why build it?

The brief was to explore a what-if: a Latinate language that survived in isolation in East Asia, reshaped by Sinitic contact, and forced by suppression into a defensive orthography. The result is a language that looks French on the page, sounds Latin in the mouth, and hides its grammar in its diacritics.

Two design constraints shaped everything:

It must be easy to read aloud, and hard to write from hearing alone.

That single rule became the language’s signature difficulty — the trait that made it notorious in-world, and the trait that makes it interesting as a conlang. The diacritics encode lexical and historical information that pronunciation does not reveal. Reading is phonetic. Writing is lexical. Every rule in the documentation flows from this asymmetry.

Two dialects, one family

Vaskric is the Standard Dialect — the Western branch. It uses the native Latin script with a productive diacritic layer, keeps the full consonant inventory, and preserves the archaic High Register intact.

Vaskoryŏ is the Eastern Dialect — the Korean branch. It uses an adapted Hangul script, has undergone the same Great Consonant Shift as Vaskric but then kept going: final voiced stops devoice, initial r becomes n, final r becomes l, and complex onsets simplify. The two dialects are mutually intelligible — the relationship is closer to Spanish and Portuguese than to, say, English and Japanese.

What you’ll find here

The archive is organised by dialect, and then by topic:

  • Phonology & orthography — the sound system, the diacritic layer, the writing direction, the two-script split
  • Grammar — nouns, pronouns, the tense system, the ergative alignment, the registers
  • Lexicon — core vocabulary with IPA, sample roots with affixation, the loanword strata (Latinate core, Sinitic substrate, Mandarin/Cantonese adstrate)
  • Texts — scriptural passages, poems, songs, and everyday dialogues in both registers
  • Culture — names, days of the week, months, idioms, registers, and the sociolinguistic history
  • Comparison — side-by-side tables for the two dialects
