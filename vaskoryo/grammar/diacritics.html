The Signature Difficulty, in Hangul

Phonetic to read — lexical to write, even in a new script

Vaskoryŏ inherited the same lexical diacritic principle as Vaskric. The exact same asymmetry holds: reading is phonetic, writing is lexical. Every mark tells you how to pronounce the word, but which mark a word carries is a matter of lexicon and history, not pronunciation. What changed is the shape of the marks — the principle is intact.

This page is the mirror of the Vaskric diacritics page, showing how the same underlying system was rendered into Hangul. Read the two pages side by side and the survival of the principle is visible.

❧ ❧ ❧

I. The Hangul modifier system

Standard Hangul cannot carry Vaskric’s diacritics directly. The Vaskoryŏns adapted the vowel system itself into a parallel modifier layer. Each Vaskric diacritic has a Hangul rendering:

Vaskric mark Function Vaskoryŏ Hangul rendering Example
acute (´) transitive subject / active doubled vowel á → 아아
grave (`) intransitive subject / patient vowel + ㆆ à → 아ㆆ
circumflex (̂) locative / lost consonant length marker (ː) â → 아ː
diaeresis (¨) plural / collective ㅇ separator aï → 아이
tilde (˜) negation / opposition final ㅇ (nasal) ã → 앙
elision (') glottal stop / morpheme boundary ㅇ with doubled mark a'it → 아이잇
macron (¯) tone on Sinitic loans only tone numeral chā¹ → 차¹

The mapping is systematic. Every Vaskric diacritic has exactly one Hangul rendering. The Vaskoryŏn writer does not have to make a choice at each word — the choice was made once, at the level of the whole script.

❧ ❧ ❧

II. Minimal pairs in Hangul

The same minimal pairs that distinguish Vaskric words distinguish Vaskoryŏ words in Hangul. The visual difference is total; the functional difference is identical.

Consonant minimal pairs

Latin Hangul IPA Meaning
tak 탁 /tak/ so
t’ak 탸크 /tʲak/ roof
sol 솔 /sol/ floor
s’ol 숄 /sʲol/ sun
nam 남 /nam/ name
ñam 냠 /ɲam/ food

Both readers, Vaskric and Vaskoryŏn, see the same distinctions. One reads them in Latin, the other in Hangul.

Vowel minimal pairs (grave vs acute)

Latin Hangul IPA Meaning
més 메스 /mes/ month
mès 메ㆆ스 /mɛs/ more

In Vaskric, the difference is in the diacritic on the vowel. In Vaskoryŏ, the difference is in the separator after the vowel. Both are lexical, both are obligatory, both carry meaning.

❧ ❧ ❧

III. The circumflex in Hangul

The circumflex marks a historically lost consonant. In Vaskoryŏ, it is rendered as a length marker because the lost consonant is gone and the vowel is simply long. The Hangul form shows the length; the lost consonant must still be learned per word.

Latin Hangul IPA Meaning Lost consonant
vós 보ː스 /voːs/ voice k (older voks)
hót 호ː스 /hoːt/ high s (older host)
pát 파ː스 /paːt/ path n (older pant)

The Hangul form of vós is 보ː스 — the vowel is long (marked), the k is gone, and the word means voice. No Hangul reader could predict from the spelling alone that the word once had a k. The circumflex-as-length is a historical flag, not a phonetic one.

❧ ❧ ❧

IV. The shibboleth thesis, in the east

The same survival strategy that made Vaskric’s diacritic layer a boundary marker applies to Vaskoryŏ, but with a different political history.

Vaskoryŏ was less visible in public life than Vaskric. The community lived in the mountains, surrounded by Korean-speakers, and used Korean for everything that faced outward. Vaskoryŏ itself was the private language of the household. The diacritic layer, rendered in Hangul, became a way of preserving a private orthography that outsiders could see but not easily reproduce.

A Korean-literate official looking at a page of Vaskoryŏ would see Hangul letters — but the doubled vowels, the ㆆ separators, and the length marks were not part of the Korean orthography they knew. The script was familiar, but the writing was not. This is the eastern equivalent of the western shibboleth: the marks look like something you know, and then they don’t.

The west kept its diacritics visible and hard to transcribe. The east kept its diacritics invisible to the untrained eye — familiar Hangul letters arranged in unfamiliar ways, a language you thought you could read until you tried to write it down.
❧ ❧ ❧

V. Two scripts, one principle

A reader who knows Vaskric can learn to read Vaskoryŏ in a few hours, because the underlying rules are identical. A reader who knows neither can look at a Vaskoryŏ page and see Korean, without realising the diacritics are doing the same work that the accent marks are doing in Vaskric.

This is the core lesson: the script is not the language. The signature difficulty is not in the letters. It is in the relationship between sound and meaning, which both dialects preserve.

Two scripts. One principle. You can always pronounce a Vaskoryŏ word you see. You cannot always spell a Vaskoryŏ word you hear. This is the family’s inheritance.
