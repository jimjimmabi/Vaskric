History of Vaskoryŏ

The Mongol breakaway and centuries of survival in the Korean interior

The Vaskoryŏns are the eastern branch of the Vaskrin diaspora. Where the Vaskric community remained in the rural areas outside Beijing — the western heartland — the Vaskoryŏns broke away during the Mongol invasions of the thirteenth century and settled in the Korean peninsula’s mountainous interior. There they remained isolated for centuries, surrounded by Korean-speaking communities, and there they reshaped their language in ways that neither the Latin-speaking west nor the Korean-speaking neighbours could ever mistake for their own.

This page tells the story of the eastern branch on its own terms. For the west, see the Vaskric history.

❧ ❧ ❧

I. The Mongol breakaway

The split was not clean. It happened over decades, in stages, in the middle of a war. When the Mongol invasions swept through northern China in the 1230s and 1240s, the Vaskrin community — then still a single, Latinate-speaking group in the rural areas outside the future Beijing — was scattered. Some families stayed. Some fled westward. Some fled south, through the Korean border, and disappeared into the mountains.

The group that fled south is the group that would become the Vaskoryŏns. They left because they were afraid, and they stayed where they landed because the mountains were safe. What was supposed to be a temporary refuge became a homeland. Within two generations, the Vaskoryŏn community had stopped thinking of itself as an exiled branch of a larger Vaskrin people and had started thinking of itself as its own thing.

The east did not set out to become a separate people. It only set out not to die. The separation was the price of survival.
❧ ❧ ❧

II. Isolation and Korean contact

Once in the Korean interior, the Vaskoryŏns were cut off from the western community — but they were surrounded by Korean-speaking villages. The isolation was geographic, not demographic. They were not alone; they were foreigners among neighbours.

This is the crucial difference from the Vaskric experience. The Vaskric community was reshaped by Cantonese and Mandarin contact, across a porous but persistent border. The Vaskoryŏn community was reshaped by Korean contact, inside the Korean state, over centuries, with no way out.

Korean left its marks everywhere:

  • Phonology — the vowel ŏ /ʌ/ entered the language. Final consonants, already simplified in Vaskric, softened further in Vaskoryŏ.
  • Prosody — pitch accent shifted toward Korean patterns; the language began to sound “Korean” in the mouth even when the words were Latin.
  • Syntax — some Korean word-order tendencies entered the everyday register, especially in embedded clauses and questions.
  • Script — the most visible change of all: the community adopted Hangul as its primary writing system.

But the Latinate core survived. The vocabulary, the grammar, and the diacritic principle all remained recognisably Vaskrin. The east is not a Korean dialect with Latin loanwords. It is a Latinate language wearing Korean clothes.

❧ ❧ ❧

III. The Great Consonant Shift of the east

Where the Vaskric dialect preserved the western branch of the Great Consonant Shift (Latin f → h word-initially; Latin kw → p; etc.), the Vaskoryŏ dialect carried those same shifts and then went further. Four additional shifts define the eastern branch:

Change Example Vaskoryŏ form
Final voiced stops devoice b → p, d → t, g → k libren → … 리프렌 /libɾen/
Initial r → n rīren → … 니렌 /niɾen/
Final r → l vaskrar → … 바스크랄 /baskɨral/
Complex onsets simplify vaskr- → bask- vaskra → … 바스카 /baskʰa/

These four shifts are what make Vaskoryŏ audibly different from Vaskric to a listener who knows both. A Vaskric speaker hearing 바스카 /baskʰa/ will recognise the root behind it (vaskra), but the sound has moved east.

❧ ❧ ❧

IV. A different kind of suppression

The Vaskoryŏns were a minority twice over: a Latinate community inside the Korean state, and a mountain community inside a lowland-dominant culture. Suppression took a different shape than it did in the west.

Where the Vaskric community in China faced explicit campaigns to register, relocate, and assimilate, the Vaskoryŏn community in Korea faced something quieter and more pervasive: the gradual gravitational pull of the surrounding language. Korean was the language of the market, the school, the court, and the temple. Vaskoryŏ was the language of home.

This produced a different kind of diglossia. The Vaskoryŏns did not preserve an explicit High Register the way the Vaskric community did — they preserved the private register, the register of the kitchen and the mountain, and they let Korean take over the public sphere.

The result is a language that is:

  • Deeply private — spoken inside the household, rarely written in public
  • Highly vernacular — the vocabulary is household vocabulary, the grammar is conversational grammar
  • Less formally differentiated than Vaskric — no elaborate High Register, no extensive scriptural tradition
  • More Korean-influenced in the syntax of everyday speech
The west preserved its liturgy. The east preserved its lullabies.
❧ ❧ ❧

V. The Hangul adoption

The most consequential cultural decision the Vaskoryŏns made was to adopt Hangul as their primary script. The reasons are not fully documented — the community kept no chronicles — but the pattern is clear.

Latin script with diacritics was a script for a community that could print. The Vaskoryŏns could not print; they could only write. They lived in the mountains, far from any press, and they wrote by hand. Hangul was easier to write by hand than any Latin adaptation. It was also easier to teach to children, and it was already the script of the neighbours. There was no reason not to use it.

What the Vaskoryŏns did not do was abandon the diacritic principle. The Hangul adaptation preserves the lexical asymmetry of the language: reading is phonetic, writing is lexical. A reader can pronounce any word they see. A listener cannot always spell a word they hear. This is the signature difficulty of the whole Vaskrin family, and the east kept it intact.

❧ ❧ ❧

VI. The east today

Vaskoryŏ is endangered — perhaps more so than Vaskric. The community is smaller, the mountains are emptying, and the pull of Korean is stronger than ever. The number of fluent speakers is unknown, but estimates put it in the low hundreds. The oldest speakers were born in the 1930s and 1940s.

What survives is a language that has never been written down systematically until this archive. Its grammar has never been formally described. Its vocabulary has never been collected. Its poems and songs have never been transcribed. This archive is the first attempt to do any of that.

If Vaskoryŏ survives the next fifty years, it will be because the community itself chose to keep it. This archive can support that choice, but it cannot replace it.

VII. Vaskric and Vaskoryŏ — a family portrait

Feature Vaskric (west) Vaskoryŏ (east)
Where Rural areas outside Beijing Mountainous Korean interior
Breakaway — (remained) During the Mongol invasions, 13th c.
Main contact language Cantonese, then Mandarin Korean
Primary script Latin + diacritics Adapted Hangul
Registers High / Neutral / Contact Mainly private vs. Korean-dominant public
Signature difficulty Lexical diacritics Lexical diacritics, Hangul-adapted

Two branches of one family. Neither is a parent to the other. Both are heirs to the same Latinate root, and both have paid for it in their own ways.
