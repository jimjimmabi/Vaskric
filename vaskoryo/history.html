History of Vaskoryŏ

Migration · Korean Contact · Suppression · Survival

The Vaskoryŏns are the eastern branch of the Vaskrin diaspora. Their story begins with the same Roman-era settlers who reached East Asia, but it diverges sharply during the Mongol invasions of the 13th century, when they fled east into the Korean peninsula. There, they remained isolated for centuries, surrounded by Korean-speaking communities, and their language was profoundly reshaped by that contact.

This is the history of how a small, isolated community in the mountains of Korea preserved its Latinate core while becoming, in sound and rhythm, unmistakably Eastern.

❧ ❧ ❧

I. The Age of Arrival

The Long Journey

In the twilight of the Roman Empire, a small group of merchants, soldiers, and their families set out from the eastern provinces. They followed the ancient trade routes that connected the Mediterranean to the mysterious lands of the East. Their journey was long and perilous, but they carried with them the most precious cargo: their language, a form of Vulgar Latin.

As they traveled further east, they became increasingly isolated from the Roman world they had left behind. The collapse of long-distance trade routes eventually severed all contact with their homeland.

The First Settlement

After years of wandering, they found refuge in a fertile valley, far from the chaos of the collapsing empires. Here, they established a small, self-sufficient community. In this isolation, their Latin began to evolve in ways it never would have in the Mediterranean.

This early community was the ancestor of both the Vaskric and Vaskoryō peoples.

❧ ❧ ❧

II. The Age of Isolation

The Sinitic Turn

As centuries passed, the Vaskrin community grew and prospered. But their isolation was not complete. They were surrounded by powerful neighbors: the vast and ancient civilization of China. Sinitic languages and culture began to exert a profound influence on the Vaskrins.

This was the "Sinitic Turn." The Vaskrin people adopted many aspects of Chinese culture: agricultural techniques, architectural styles, and, most importantly, vocabulary. The language absorbed a massive influx of Sinitic loanwords, particularly for concepts the original Latin lacked.

The Diacritic Strategy

As the language became more complex, the Vaskrins developed a unique writing system. They adapted the Latin script, but added a layer of diacritics to mark subtle differences in pronunciation and grammar. This system, which looked like French on the page, was actually a sophisticated tool for preserving their linguistic identity. It was a deliberate strategy of divergence from the surrounding Sinitic languages.

❧ ❧ ❧

III. The Age of Divergence

The Mongol Breakaway

The peace of the Vaskrin valleys was shattered by the Mongol invasions of the 13th century. The Mongol conquests brought chaos and destruction. The Vaskrin community, which had remained largely intact for centuries, was split apart.

One faction, seeking safety, fled south and west, deeper into the mountains. They became the ancestors of the modern Vaskric people. They preserved the Latinate core of the language, but their isolation from the eastern branch led to a different set of linguistic changes.

The other faction, more adventurous or perhaps more desperate, fled east. They crossed the mountains and eventually settled in the Korean peninsula. This was the birth of the Vaskoryō dialect.

The Eastern Settlement

The Vaskoryōns settled in the mountainous interior of the Korean peninsula. Here, they were isolated from the Vaskric community but intimately surrounded by Korean-speaking communities. This was the crucible in which their language was forged.

Over centuries of contact with Korean, their language underwent a profound transformation. The sounds softened. Final voiced stops devoiced. The trilled /r/ softened. The unique Vaskoryō vowel, /ə/, was born from Korean influence. The grammar shifted, adopting some Korean patterns of topic-comment structure.

The Two Dialects

The divergence was not total. The two branches remained mutually intelligible, akin to Spanish and Portuguese. A Standard Vaskric speaker and a Vaskoryōn speaker can converse with modest effort after a few days of exposure.

But the differences were significant. The western branch preserved the full Latinate consonant clusters, like vaskr-. The eastern branch simplified them, turning vaskr- into bask-. The western branch kept final r; the eastern branch turned it into l. The western branch kept the trilled /r/; the eastern branch softened it.

❧ ❧ ❧

IV. The Age of Suppression

The Hidden Centuries

The centuries that followed were difficult for both branches of the Vaskrin people. In the East, the Vaskoryōns were a small community in a foreign land. They were often discriminated against and marginalized. To survive, they developed a "Public/Private split." In public, they spoke Korean; in private, they preserved their own language.

But their resilience was remarkable. They preserved their language, their customs, and their history, passing them down from generation to generation.

Diglossia and Registers

That history produced a diglossia with three registers:

Register Form Domain
High Liturgical, archaic, VSO, éd, prophetic fronting Scripture, formal poetry, legal declarations, ceremonies
Neutral Standard SVO, everyday written and formal spoken Vaskoryō Education, administration, published prose
Contact Topic-comment order, serial verbs, optional classifiers, Korean loans, aspect over tense Everyday spoken life, especially rapid or informal speech

A fourth, covert register existed historically: an in-group way of speaking used to evade monitoring, characterised by euphemism, indirection, and the full lexical diacritic layer in writing. This is the context in which the diacritic shibboleth emerged.

Taboo and Euphemism

Suppressed minorities develop euphemistic vocabulary for authorities, informants, and forbidden topics. Vaskoryō has a small taboo lexicon, used in the covert register:

Vaskoryō IPA Literal Euphemistic meaning
l’okren longen /lʔokren longen/ the long eye the authorities
l’lingua brefen /lʔlingwa brefen/ the short tongue an informant
l’portren apert /lʔportren apert/ the open door a raid
l’pulvis /lʔpulvis/ the dust disappearance, death
l’silentia /lʔsilentia/ the silence a period of suppression

These expressions are not used in the High Register or in formal writing; they belong to the covert register and to oral tradition.

V. The Modern Era

Endangerment and Revitalisation

Vaskoryō is endangered. The community's language transmission was interrupted during the suppression periods, and today the number of fluent speakers is small — a few thousand at most, concentrated in the older generation.

Revitalisation efforts are recent and fragile: a community-based literacy programme, a small corpus of recorded oral literature, and the beginnings of a school curriculum. This archive is itself part of the revitalisation effort: it documents the language for learners and for the community's own future.
